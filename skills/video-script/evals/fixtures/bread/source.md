# Dough development and leavening: technical notes

From a baking-science course handout.

- Baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments fermentable sugars, chiefly glucose and maltose. Maltose is liberated from damaged starch by the flour's own amylase enzymes. Through glycolysis and alcoholic fermentation the yeast yields carbon dioxide and ethanol.
- On hydration, the flour proteins glutenin and gliadin bond into gluten. Mechanical work (kneading) aligns the gluten into a viscoelastic network: it stretches under gas pressure and springs back.
- The gluten network retains CO2 in discrete gas cells; as fermentation continues, the cells inflate and the dough's volume increases (proofing).
- In the oven, the gas cells expand with heat and ethanol vaporises, producing a final rapid rise known as oven spring during the first minutes of baking.
- Yeast is killed at around 60 °C. Between about 60 and 80 °C, starch gelatinises and gluten proteins coagulate, setting the crumb structure around the gas cells, so the loaf holds its volume after it cools.
