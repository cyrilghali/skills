# Video script reference

Two parts: the rules `SKILL.md` follows, each with the published source behind it, and the claim check.

## Rules and their sources

Status marks: `confirmed` (two independent sources state it), `single-source`, `inferred` (reasoned from a stated premise, not tested directly), `contested` (sources disagree). Every link was read on 27/10/2026.

Most of the learning evidence comes from short, system-paced lessons for novices, much of it from Richard Mayer and his co-authors. It backs the shape of these rules. It says little about durations and word counts, so those numbers live in `STYLE.md`.

| Rule | Status | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Words on screen are a few key words, never the narration.** Two or three printed words that repeat the narration's words, placed next to the matching part of the picture, improved recall. Text identical to the narration hurt learning. | `confirmed` | [Mayer & Johnson 2008](https://openalex.org/W1992439949); [Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/YueBjorkBjork2013_redundancy.pdf) (citing the Adesope & Nesbit 2012 meta-analysis); [Brame 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/) |
| **The voice explains; the screen shows pictures and labels** (modality). | `confirmed` | [Mayer 2020 principle list](https://ugc.futurelearn.com/uploads/files/7d/d6/7dd6188d-c343-4311-b064-ac98d2c95abc/Multimedia_Principles._R._E._Mayer__2020.pdf); Brame 2016 |
| **Say the words about a picture while it appears or moves, and put each label next to the thing it names** (temporal and spatial contiguity). | temporal `single-source`; spatial `confirmed` | Mayer 2020 list; [spatial contiguity meta-analysis 2026](https://openalex.org/W7214897304) (28 studies, g = 0.73) |
| **Mark each new part with a short cue** (signaling). | `confirmed` | Mayer 2020 list; Brame 2016 |
| **Cut interesting but irrelevant detail, hardest at the start** (coherence; "seductive details"). | `confirmed` | [Harp & Mayer 1998](https://openalex.org/W2125578959); Mayer 2020 list; Brame 2016 |
| **Raise the question before the fact that answers it.** A question the viewer cannot answer yet, asked before a short video, improved recall. | `confirmed` for short videos; on 20-minute lectures the gain held for the questioned items only | [Loewenstein 1994](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/); [Carpenter & Toftness 2017, as reported by the BPS](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/heres-simple-way-boost-your-learning-videos-prequestion); [Toftness et al. 2018](https://openalex.org/W2885793954) |
| **Put a list on screen as labels; the voice says the item that matters now.** | `inferred` from coherence and the key-word finding | Mayer & Johnson 2008; Mayer 2020 list |
| **Show a worked case before naming the general rule.** | `contested`: concrete-first and abstract-first did equally well in one test | [3Blue1Brown FAQ](https://www.3blue1brown.com/faq/) (the method's author); [Fyfe & Nathan 2018](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/2c814639-f4c7-4091-a6eb-5e6e834bc546/content); [concreteness fading in physics, 2021](https://openalex.org/W3186050527) |
| **Link beats by cause ("so") or contrast ("but"), not "and then".** Contrastive and causal connectives raised comprehension; additive ones lowered it. | rule `confirmed`; research `single-source` | Trey Parker and Matt Stone, NYU class ([transcript](https://speakola.com/arts/matt-stone-trey-parker-nyu-writing-class-2014), [report](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2014/03/06/writing-advice-from-south-parks-trey-parker-and-matt-stone/)); [Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders 2019](https://utrecht-main-test.atmire.com/items/3b04a191-6ab7-4a63-a3be-a53ab7880311) |
| **When the topic has an actor, tell it as that actor pursuing one goal.** Narratives were recalled better than expository texts (g = .55). | `single-source` (meta-analysis) | [Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/) |
| **State the wrong idea the viewer probably holds, then show where it fails.** Videos that refuted common misconceptions gave the largest gains (d = 0.79 and 0.83). | `single-source`, large effect | [Muller et al. 2008](https://openalex.org/W1784274060) |
| **Speak to the viewer as "you", in a conversational tone.** | `confirmed`; the effect faded in lessons over 35 minutes | [Ginns, Martin & Marsh 2013](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/designing-instructional-text-in-a-conversational-style-a-meta-ana/); Mayer 2020 list; Brame 2016 |
| **Define a new term in plain words the first time it is spoken; skip the definition for an audience that already uses the term.** | `inferred` from pre-training plus contiguity; expertise reversal `single-source` | Mayer 2020 list; [Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://openalex.org/W2001468118) |
| **Short, active sentences a listener takes in on one hearing, under about 20 words.** | practitioner consensus `confirmed`; no controlled study of sentence length for listeners | [The News Manual](https://mediahelpingmedia.org/basics/journalism-language-and-style-basics/); [GOV.UK, 2014](https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2014/08/04/sentence-length-why-25-words-is-our-limit/) |
| **Length.** Keep a video well under six minutes. Plan at about 2.6 spoken words a second (156 a minute), a planning figure, not a ceiling. | under six minutes `confirmed` for watch time, not learning; 2.6 words a second `single-source` | [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf) (mean 156 words a minute); Brame 2016 |
| **Split a long topic into short separate parts the viewer can pause between, each answering one question** (segmenting). | `confirmed` for user-paced parts; weak for chapters inside one continuous video | Mayer 2020 list; Brame 2016 |
| **Hand-off note: a friendly human or human-like voice.** | `single-source` | Mayer 2020 list |
| **Optional: end with one question the viewer can try to answer.** | `single-source` | Mayer 2020 list (generative activity); Brame 2016 |

Burned-in captions identical to the narration hurt in these studies. Subtitles a viewer can turn on are a separate track; the studies did not test them.

## Check every claim

This part is an editorial process, not a learning finding. It exists because a viewer cannot check a spoken fact: a wrong fact in the voice lands as true.

A claim is any statement of fact the narration makes: a count, a size, a date, a default, a name, a cause, a sequence of events, a comparison, and any "only", "always", "never" or "-est". A premise in the request or the brief is a claim like any other. Each claim names a source another person could open and reach the same verdict.

| Topic | A source that counts | Not a source |
|---|---|---|
| A codebase | `path:line` at the commit you read | the function name alone |
| A spec, paper or book | the document and its section or page | the title alone |
| The wider world | a page from the body that holds the fact (an agency, a national archive, a standards body, a peer-reviewed paper), with its URL | a quiz site, a forum answer, a page that cites nothing |
| Data | the dataset, the query and the result | a figure remembered from a dashboard |
| A behavior you can run | the command and the output it printed | what the command should print |
| A person | their name, role and the date they confirmed the wording | "the team says" |

Memory, the request itself and "everyone knows" are not sources.

**`claims.json`** is a list with one entry per claim:

```json
[{"beat": "expire", "fact": "f03", "claim": "A cached page expires after five minutes", "source": "cache.md, Expiry", "verdict": "true"},
 {"beat": "refresh", "claim": "A stale page is refreshed in the background", "source": "https://example.com/docs#refresh", "verdict": "partly", "note": "only while the origin answers"}]
```

- `fact` holds the brief's fact id when the claim says a brief fact. `claims.py --brief` fails for each must-say fact no entry names.
- Verdicts: `true` (the source says so), `partly` (true with a limit; `note` says which part holds), `false` (the source says otherwise), `outdated` (true once, no longer), `unverifiable` (no source settles it, after trying each kind that could).
- Fix a `false` or `outdated` claim in the narration. Cut an `unverifiable` claim, or reword it so it asserts nothing; "usually" is not enough, so drop the number or the name. Keep the old verdict in the entry: the script requires the beat to have changed.
- Run `scripts/claims.py <folder> --stamp` right after judging and before editing: it records each beat's text as checked.

**What the script checks.** `beats.json` must be a list of `id` and `text` objects with unique ids, and its text must hold no digit or symbol the voice would misread. A beat that holds a number (digits or words), a CamelCase name, a file name or an absolute word ("only", "never", "largest") needs a claims entry. One entry covers the whole beat for this check, so list each claim the beat makes yourself. A claim with no such token (a cause, a sequence) still needs an entry; the second read catches the ones you missed.

**The second read.** Run `scripts/claims.py <folder> --blind > blind-claims.json`. It keeps only each entry's beat, claim and source. Hand a reviewer that has not seen your reasoning `beats.json`, `blind-claims.json` and the sources: a subagent if the host offers one, else a new session. Never hand it `claims.json`, `script.md` or your notes. It opens each source and writes `second-read.json`: one `{"beat", "claim", "verdict"}` per entry, beat and claim copied unchanged, plus an entry for each claim in `beats.json` that has none. Never edit that file yourself. Where its verdict differs from yours, open the source, set the right verdict in `claims.json` and write the reason in `resolved`. Add each claim it found. `scripts/claims.py <folder> --final` fails until all of that is done. When the host offers neither a subagent nor a new session, do the second read yourself from `blind-claims.json` alone, and say so in the report.

## Remaking a narration

A remake loses facts when the trim is done by feel. Before writing the new beats, list every claim of the old version from its captions or transcript, never from memory, one row each in `remake.md`:

| Old claim | Where (timestamp) | Status | Why, or where now |
|---|---|---|---|

Status is one of `kept` (same beat), `moved` (another beat; name it), `dropped on purpose` (the reason is mandatory: out of scope, wrong, outdated) or `dropped by accident`. A row is `kept` or `moved` only when the new narration itself states the claim, with every detail the old one had. A claim left to the picture or a note is `dropped on purpose`, with that as its reason. Restore every accidental drop. After the new beats exist, mark each row again; no row stays blank. Check the new version's claims against their sources as usual.

When the old version is a video with burned-in captions, read the captions from its frames instead of transcribing the audio.
