# ENV 214 — Groundwater: lecturer's source notes

Notes for building the groundwater module of a second-year environmental science course. Most students take it as a required unit. Students on the hydrology track also sit an extra assessed problem set that needs the quantitative parts.

## Core ideas

Groundwater is water held in the pore spaces and cracks of soil and rock below the water table.

The water table is the surface below which the ground is saturated. Above it, in the unsaturated zone, pores hold both air and water.

An aquifer is a body of rock or sediment that stores groundwater and lets it flow out in useful amounts. Sand, gravel and fractured limestone make good aquifers; clay does not.

An unconfined aquifer is open to the surface through the unsaturated zone. A confined aquifer lies between layers of low-permeability rock.

Water in a confined aquifer is under pressure, so it rises in a well above the top of the aquifer. If it rises above the ground, the well is artesian.

Recharge is water that reaches the water table, mostly from rain soaking down. Discharge is water that leaves the aquifer, into rivers, springs, wetlands or wells.

Groundwater supplies roughly a third of the drinking water used worldwide, and a larger share in many dry regions.

Groundwater moves slowly, usually between a few centimetres and a few metres per day.

Pumping a well lowers the water table around it in a cone of depression. Neighbouring wells inside the cone can run dry.

When pumping is greater than recharge over years, the water table falls. In fine sediments this can make the land surface sink, called subsidence, and the lost storage does not come back.

Near the coast, heavy pumping can draw seawater into a freshwater aquifer.

Contaminants spread in a plume that follows the direction of groundwater flow.

Cleaning a contaminated aquifer is slow and expensive, and often takes decades; preventing contamination is much cheaper.

## Quantitative part (hydrology track)

Porosity is the fraction of a material's volume that is empty space.

Hydraulic conductivity, K, measures how easily water moves through a material, in metres per day.

Hydraulic head is the height to which water rises in a well, measured from a common reference level. Water flows from high head to low head.

The hydraulic gradient is the change in head divided by the distance between the two points.

Darcy's law: the flow per unit area, q, equals K times the hydraulic gradient (q = −K dh/dl).

The actual speed of the water through the pores is q divided by the effective porosity, so it is faster than q.

Clay can have a high porosity but a very low K, because its pores are tiny and badly connected.

Darcy's law holds only for slow, smooth (laminar) flow, which covers almost all natural groundwater flow except in large karst conduits.

## Notes to self

- Do not repeat the textbook's line that groundwater mostly flows in "underground rivers". That is a misconception: almost all groundwater moves through small pores and cracks. Open conduits exist only in some karst limestone.
- The course handbook still lists "groundwater supplies half of world drinking water". That figure is outdated; current estimates are about a third.
